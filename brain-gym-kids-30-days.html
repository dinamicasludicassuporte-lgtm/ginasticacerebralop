Before you start

How to use this guide

This guide brings together 120 activities organized into a 30-day routine. Every day, your child will practice 4 short activities — one from each category — in about 7 to 15 minutes total. No special equipment or prior experience is needed: any adult can lead it.

📅 Program structure

  • 30 days, organized into 5 weeks of 6 days.
  • 4 activities per day — one from each of the 4 categories.
  • Each activity takes between 1 and 10 minutes.
  • Can be done in any order throughout the day.

⏰ When to practice

  • Pick a fixed time — ideally away from moments of crisis.
  • Good windows: right after waking up, after school, before homework.
  • Avoid using it as a punishment or right after a meltdown.
  • A predictable schedule helps more than a perfect activity.

🧩 If your child refuses

  • Don't force it — offer, invite, demonstrate it yourself first.
  • Change the order: start with the activity they enjoy most.
  • It's fine to skip a day; pick back up the next day, guilt-free.
  • Turn it into a game, not a mandatory chore.

✅ Every activity includes

  • Age range, duration and required materials.
  • A simple step-by-step on how to lead it.
  • Why it works — the reasoning behind the exercise.
  • An extra tip for tougher days.
An important note: this guide is a playful complement to your family routine and does not replace evaluation, diagnosis or follow-up care from health professionals. If anger outbursts, hyperactivity or defiance are very intense, frequent, or are disrupting school and family life, seek support from a pediatrician, child psychologist or occupational therapist.

🧺 Suggested basic kit (optional)

The vast majority of activities need nothing beyond the body itself. These few items, if available at home, make some days even richer:

  • Bubble solution and wand
  • Modeling dough (a firm one, like theraputty)
  • A sock ball or soft small ball
  • A heavy blanket or weighted pillow
  • Painter's tape or chalk to draw lines on the floor
  • A clear jar, water, glue and glitter (calm-down jar)
The 4 categories

Where the activities come from

The 120 activities were organized into 4 categories that complement each other. Every day of the routine includes one activity from each of them, forming a complete package for the body and the emotions.

🔀

Coordination & Midline

Movements that cross the body from one side to the other, helping both brain hemispheres work together — the foundation for reading, writing, paying attention and organizing one's own body in space.

💪

Rhythm & Strength

'Heavy work' activities that push, pull and press through the joints — the most effective way to organize the body and prevent meltdowns before they start.

🎈

Balance & Emotions

Body balance and emotional awareness go hand in hand: when the body feels safe and steady, it becomes much easier to name and move through big feelings without exploding.

🌬️

Calm & Focus

Breathing and attention games activate the parasympathetic nervous system and train executive functions — the pair that underlies self-control in the face of tantrums and defiance.

About the sources: the activities draw on practices widely used in Occupational Therapy, Sensory Integration (Ayres), psychomotor education, mindfulness for children, kids' yoga, and the popular movements from the Brain Gym® method (Dennison) — used here purely as coordination and midline-crossing exercises, without any claim of a specific neurological effect. They also draw on ideas from authors like Daniel Siegel & Tina Payne Bryson ("name it to tame it") and Bruce Perry on regulating emotion through the body.

Today we start slow: a simple coordination move, a rhythmic foot tap, a comfortable weight on the lap, and the first tense-and-release muscle exercise.
🔀Coordination

Cross Crawl

Age: 3–10 yearsDuration: 3 to 5 minutes
Standing up, the child marches very slowly, touching the left knee with the right hand and then the right knee with the left hand.
  1. Have the child stand with arms relaxed at their sides.
  2. Marching very slowly, they touch the left knee with the right hand.
  3. Switch sides: touch the right knee with the left hand, repeating for 1 to 2 minutes.
Why it works: The alternating movement requires both sides of the body to communicate with each other, exercising the motor planning that also supports attention and self-control.
Tip for tough days: If the child is very worked up, start extra slow and count out loud together — the shared rhythm alone starts to calm things down.
Materials: No materials needed
💪Strength & Proprioception

Alternating Foot Taps

Age: 3–6 yearsDuration: 2 minutes
Seated, the child marches by tapping their feet on the floor alternately, following a simple rhythm.
  1. Seat the child in a chair with feet free.
  2. Have them tap the left foot and then the right foot on the floor, at a steady rhythm.
  3. Sing or count along to keep the rhythm for 1 to 2 minutes.
Why it works: A simple, discreet movement that helps channel foot restlessness, common in young children during moments of waiting.
Tip for tough days: Great to use at the table before meals, when the child struggles to sit still.
Materials: No materials needed
🎈Emotional Regulation

Weighted Pillow on the Lap

Age: 3–8 yearsDuration: 5 to 10 minutes
The child holds a comfortable weight on their lap (weighted pillow, folded blanket) while listening to a calm story.
  1. Choose a weighted pillow, a well-folded blanket or a comfortable rice bag.
  2. Sit the child down and place the weight on their lap.
  3. Read a calm story while they feel the weight, without rushing.
Why it works: The constant weight on the lap provides continuous proprioceptive input, helping the child stay seated and regulated for longer.
Tip for tough days: Use this resource during difficult waiting moments, like doctor visits or long trips.
Materials: A weighted pillow or a folded blanket
🌬️Breathing & Focus

Squeeze the Lemon (Progressive Muscle Relaxation)

Age: 5–10 yearsDuration: 3 to 5 minutes
The child squeezes and releases muscle groups of the body (hands, arms, shoulders) as if squeezing imaginary lemons.
  1. Have the child close their hands with force, as if squeezing a lemon, for 5 seconds.
  2. Release all at once, feeling the difference between tension and relaxation.
  3. Repeat with the arms, shoulders and legs, one muscle group at a time.
Why it works: Progressive muscle relaxation teaches the body to tell tension apart from relaxation, a key skill for calming down actively rather than just passively.
Tip for tough days: Use before bed if the child struggles to relax their body at night.
Materials: No materials needed

The body learns by crossing elbow to knee, the hands build strength shaping dough, and butterfly breathing arrives as a first self-soothing tool.
🔀Coordination

Elbow-to-Knee

Age: 6–10 yearsDuration: 3 to 5 minutes
Like the cross crawl, but touching the elbow to the opposite knee, requiring more trunk flexion and balance.
  1. Standing up, have them bring the right elbow toward the left knee, which lifts to meet it.
  2. Return to the starting position with control, without letting the body collapse.
  3. Alternate to the other side, repeating 8 to 10 times per side.
Why it works: The trunk flexion adds a balance demand on top of the midline crossing, strengthening the core and motor self-regulation at the same time.
Tip for tough days: On very agitated days, slow it down and have the child hold each touch for 1 second — this naturally reduces the impulsiveness of the movement.
Materials: No materials needed
💪Strength & Proprioception

Rolling Dough with a Rolling Pin

Age: 3–8 yearsDuration: 5 minutes
The child uses both hands at the same time to roll dough with a rolling pin, in a symmetrical movement.
  1. Give the child a portion of dough and a small rolling pin.
  2. Have them rest both hands on the pin and push it forward and back.
  3. Repeat until the dough is well flattened, then start again with a new ball.
Why it works: The symmetrical effort of both hands against the dough's resistance provides calming proprioceptive input, useful before tasks that require concentration.
Tip for tough days: If the child is very agitated, use firmer dough (like theraputty) to demand more strength and generate a stronger calming effect.
Materials: Modeling dough and a small rolling pin
🎈Emotional Regulation

Jumping on a Mattress or Trampoline

Age: 3–10 yearsDuration: 3 to 5 minutes
Repeated jumps on a springy surface, combining intense proprioceptive and vestibular stimulation.
  1. Choose a safe spot: a trampoline, a mattress on the floor, or even a sturdy sofa with supervision.
  2. Have the child jump in a controlled way, keeping their feet together.
  3. Set a time limit (3 to 5 minutes) and give a 1-minute warning before it ends.
Why it works: Combines two of the most organizing stimuli for the nervous system — weight and movement — making it one of the fastest ways to discharge intense energy.
Tip for tough days: Giving advance notice that the activity is about to end helps avoid meltdowns when it's time to stop.
Materials: A mattress or trampoline
🌬️Breathing & Focus

Butterfly Hug

Age: 4–10 yearsDuration: 1 to 3 minutes
The child crosses their arms over the chest and gives gentle, alternating taps to their own shoulders, like butterfly wings flapping.
  1. Have the child cross their arms over the chest, hands on opposite shoulders.
  2. They give gentle, alternating taps, first on one shoulder, then the other, at a slow rhythm.
  3. Continue for 1 to 2 minutes, breathing slowly together.
Why it works: Rhythmic, self-applied bilateral touch is a technique used in trauma therapies to help calm the nervous system independently.
Tip for tough days: Teach it as a tool the child can use alone, anywhere, without needing an adult's help.
Materials: No materials needed

Today we combine trunk rotation, paired cooperation, a slow gentle swing, and a whole-body attention pause — a great combo for hectic afternoons.
🔀Coordination

Heel Touch Behind the Back

Age: 6–10 yearsDuration: 3 to 5 minutes
The child lifts a heel behind them and touches it with the opposite hand, crossing the midline with a trunk rotation.
  1. Standing up, have them bend a knee and lift the left heel behind them.
  2. The right hand reaches back and touches that heel, rotating the trunk slightly.
  3. Switch sides and repeat for about 10 reps per side.
Why it works: The trunk rotation combined with the crossing broadens body awareness and helps agitated children better feel the boundaries of their own body.
Tip for tough days: If the child loses balance easily, let them rest the other hand on a wall or chair.
Materials: No materials needed
💪Strength & Proprioception

Back-to-Back Ball Pass

Age: 5–10 yearsDuration: 5 minutes
In pairs, seated or standing back-to-back, they rotate the trunk to pass the ball from hand to hand.
  1. Position both participants back-to-back, close together.
  2. One holds the ball and rotates the trunk to pass it to the partner sideways.
  3. The partner receives it and rotates to the other side, passing the ball back around in a circle.
Why it works: The pair cooperation adds a social component to the physical exercise, helping children who struggle with sharing and waiting their turn.
Tip for tough days: If the child gets frustrated with paired games, start as their partner yourself before introducing another child or sibling.
Materials: A ball
🎈Emotional Regulation

Slow Linear Swinging

Age: 3–10 yearsDuration: 3 to 5 minutes
Rhythmic, linear swinging back and forth, at a slow, steady pace.
  1. Seat the child on a safe swing or hammock.
  2. Push gently, keeping a slow, steady rhythm, with no sudden movements.
  3. Continue for a few minutes, watching their body relax.
Why it works: Slow, linear movement is one of the most calming vestibular stimuli there is, with an almost immediate effect on anxiety and agitation.
Tip for tough days: Avoid swinging fast or irregularly — the more predictable the rhythm, the greater the calming effect.
Materials: A swing or hammock
🌬️Breathing & Focus

Body Scan

Age: 6–10 yearsDuration: 3 to 5 minutes
Lying or sitting comfortably, the child pays attention, part by part, to how each region of the body is feeling.
  1. Have the child lie or sit comfortably and close their eyes if they'd like.
  2. Slowly guide the attention: 'feel your feet... your legs... your belly... your shoulders...' up to the head.
  3. End by inviting a deep breath and a gradual return of attention to the room.
Why it works: Noticing body sensations part by part broadens interoception and teaches the child to notice physical signs of tension before they turn into meltdowns.
Tip for tough days: Record your own voice guiding the body scan so the child can listen alone whenever they need it, without depending on you being present.
Materials: No materials needed

A lazy 8 for the eyes, a ball that crosses the body's center, the steady rocking of a chair, and the first practice of STOP: pausing before reacting.
🔀Coordination

Lazy 8

Age: 5–10 yearsDuration: 2 to 3 minutes
With the arm extended, the child traces a lying-down '8' (the infinity symbol) in the air, eyes following the hand without turning the head.
  1. Have the child extend an arm forward with the thumb up.
  2. They draw a large lazy 8 in the air, starting from the center and going up to the left.
  3. The eyes follow the thumb the whole time without turning the head; repeat 3 to 5 times with each hand.
Why it works: Following one's own movement with the eyes across the center of the visual field trains smooth eye tracking, which matters for reading and sustained focus.
Tip for tough days: If the child is distracted, slow down the tracing — the slower the 8, the easier it is to sustain attention on it.
Materials: No materials needed (optional: cardboard with an 8 drawn on it)
💪Strength & Proprioception

Sock Ball Between the Hands

Age: 4–8 yearsDuration: 3 minutes
The child passes a sock ball from one hand to the other, crossing the center of the body with each pass.
  1. Place the sock ball in the child's right hand.
  2. Have them pass it to the left hand crossing behind the back.
  3. Then try passing it under the legs, alternating the routes.
Why it works: A simple game that combines midline crossing with light proprioception, easy to do anywhere, including in the car.
Tip for tough days: Turn it into a timed challenge: 'how many times can you pass it in 30 seconds?' — the timer helps keep focus.
Materials: A sock ball (or a soft small ball)
🎈Emotional Regulation

Rocking Chair

Age: 3–8 yearsDuration: 3 to 5 minutes
The child rocks seated in a rocking chair, at their own steady pace.
  1. Seat the child in a safe, stable rocking chair.
  2. Let them rock at their own pace, with no rush.
  3. Use the moment for a calm chat or simply shared silence.
Why it works: The self-regulated rhythm (they control the speed themselves) gives the child a sense of control, which by itself is emotionally regulating.
Tip for tough days: A great spot for the home 'calm-down corner' — keep the chair always available, with no strict rules about when it can be used.
Materials: A rocking chair
🌬️Breathing & Focus

STOP (Stop, Take a Breath, Observe, Proceed)

Age: 7–10 yearsDuration: 1 to 2 minutes
A simple acronym taught to the child to pause before reacting on impulse: Stop what they're doing, Take a breath, Observe what they're feeling, and only then Proceed.
  1. Teach the acronym during a calm moment: Stop what you're doing, Take a deep breath, Observe what you're feeling in body and mind, and only then Proceed with a choice.
  2. Practice STOP in neutral situations first, like a game, before expecting it to work in a real crisis.
  3. When a mild frustration comes up, gently remind them: 'remember our STOP?'
Why it works: Adding a named, formal step between the trigger and the reaction literally builds the pause that's missing in children with impulsive anger outbursts.
Tip for tough days: Never introduce this tool for the first time during a crisis — teach and practice it during calm moments so it's already available when it's needed.
Materials: No materials needed

🔀Coordination

Two-Handed Lazy 8

Age: 6–10 yearsDuration: 2 to 3 minutes
The child interlaces their hands and traces the lazy 8 in the air with both hands joined together, combining bilateral integration with visual crossing.
  1. Have them interlace the fingers of both hands, forming one joined fist.
  2. Trace the lazy 8 very slowly in the air, eyes following the hands.
  3. Repeat for about 1 minute, then switch the finger interlacing and repeat.
Why it works: Joining both hands into a single fluid movement trains cooperation between the two sides of the body, essential for fine motor tasks like handwriting.
Tip for tough days: If interlacing the fingers is hard, it's fine to hold a short stick or a large pencil with both hands instead.
Materials: No materials needed
💪Strength & Proprioception

Copy the Tabletop Sequence

Age: 5–10 yearsDuration: 4 minutes
The adult creates a short sequence of movements ('tap your head twice, tap the floor once, hands on your knees') that the child repeats from memory.
  1. Demonstrate a short 3-move sequence, for example: tap the head twice, tap the floor once, hands on the knees.
  2. Have the child repeat the sequence in the exact same order.
  3. Create a new sequence each round, gradually adding more movements.
Why it works: Memorizing and reproducing motor sequences directly trains working memory, one of the executive functions most linked to defiance when it's weak.
Tip for tough days: If the child makes a mistake, don't correct right away — let them notice on their own and try again; this builds independence instead of reliance on outside correction.
Materials: No materials needed
🎈Emotional Regulation

Single-Leg Balance

Age: 4–10 yearsDuration: 2 to 3 minutes
The child stands supported on just one leg, then switches to the other, training static balance.
  1. Have the child stand supported on just the right leg.
  2. Hold for 10 to 15 seconds, or as long as they can.
  3. Switch to the left leg and repeat, alternating a few times.
Why it works: Static balance demands total focus on the present moment, working almost like a moving meditation for young children.
Tip for tough days: Turn it into a game: 'who can stay still longest?' — the challenge helps sustain focus without it feeling like a boring exercise.
Materials: No materials needed
🌬️Breathing & Focus

Flower and Candle Breathing

Age: 3–8 yearsDuration: 1 to 2 minutes
The child imagines smelling a flower (breathing in slowly through the nose) and then blowing out a candle (breathing out slowly through the mouth).
  1. Have the child imagine holding a flower in one hand and smelling it, breathing in slowly through the nose.
  2. Then, imagining a candle in the other hand, blowing it out slowly through the mouth.
  3. Repeat the cycle 5 to 8 times, alternating flower and candle.
Why it works: One of the simplest and most effective breathing techniques for young children, since it uses concrete, easy-to-grasp images.
Tip for tough days: Use the child's own fingers as the imaginary flower and candle — no real object needed, works anywhere.
Materials: No materials needed

🔀Coordination

Alphabet 8s

Age: 6–10 yearsDuration: 4 to 5 minutes
The child traces letters from their own name or the alphabet inside the shape of the lazy 8, combining handwriting practice with visual crossing.
  1. Draw (or have them draw) a large lazy 8 on paper or in the air.
  2. Pick a letter from their name and trace that letter following the path of the 8.
  3. Repeat with other letters, always tracking with the eyes.
Why it works: Combines midline crossing with handwriting practice, helping children with focus difficulties stay engaged longer with desk tasks.
Tip for tough days: For kids who resist anything that looks like schoolwork, turn it into a game: 'let's write your name inside the magic loop.'
Materials: Paper and pencil (optional)
💪Strength & Proprioception

Crawling on All Fours

Age: 3–8 yearsDuration: 3 to 5 minutes
The child crawls normally, with the opposite arm and leg moving forward together, a classic contralateral pattern.
  1. Have the child rest hands and knees on the floor.
  2. They move the right arm forward together with the left leg, then the left arm with the right leg.
  3. Make a short course (from one piece of furniture to another) crawling.
Why it works: Crawling is one of the most fundamental motor patterns for integrating the two sides of the body, forming the base for midline crossing in other activities.
Tip for tough days: Turn it into an 'animal circuit': crawl like a dog, then like a bear — the variety keeps easily-distracted children engaged.
Materials: Open floor space
🎈Emotional Regulation

Walking the Line

Age: 3–10 yearsDuration: 3 minutes
The child walks along a straight line on the floor, with arms out to help with balance.
  1. Place a strip of tape or a stretched rope on the floor, forming a straight line.
  2. Have the child walk along the line with arms out.
  3. Repeat back and forth, gradually increasing speed.
Why it works: Walking on a narrow line demands full attention to one's own body, a simple way to anchor the child in the present moment.
Tip for tough days: Use colored tape and let the child draw their own path (curves, zigzags) to boost engagement.
Materials: A rope, tape, or a line drawn on the floor
🌬️Breathing & Focus

Balloon Breathing

Age: 3–8 yearsDuration: 1 to 2 minutes
The child imagines their belly as a balloon, filling up slowly on the in-breath and emptying slowly on the out-breath.
  1. Have the child lie down or sit and place their hands on their belly.
  2. They breathe in slowly through the nose, imagining the belly inflating like a balloon.
  3. They breathe out slowly through the mouth, feeling the belly deflate, and repeat for 5 to 8 cycles.
Why it works: Feeling the hands rise and fall with the breath helps the child physically notice their own breathing rhythm, the first step toward controlling it.
Tip for tough days: Place a light stuffed animal on the belly to make the movement even more visible and fun.
Materials: No materials needed

🔀Coordination

Double Doodle

Age: 4–10 yearsDuration: 3 to 5 minutes
The child draws mirrored shapes with both hands at the same time, each hand making the opposite movement of the other.
  1. Give the child a pencil for each hand over a large sheet of paper.
  2. Have them draw circles, waves or hearts with both hands at once, one mirroring the other.
  3. Let them explore freely for a few minutes, switching shapes.
Why it works: Working both hands at once in mirror image strengthens bilateral integration and tends to be very enjoyable, which helps regulate mood.
Tip for tough days: A great transition activity before homework: it calms without demanding much cognitive effort.
Materials: Large paper and two pencils/crayons
💪Strength & Proprioception

Air Bicycle

Age: 3–8 yearsDuration: 2 to 3 minutes
Lying on their back, the child pedals their legs in the air as if riding a bicycle.
  1. Have the child lie on their back with arms resting on the floor.
  2. They lift their legs and pedal like a bicycle, at a comfortable pace.
  3. Continue for 30 seconds to 1 minute, with short breaks if needed.
Why it works: Activates the abdominal muscles and the alternating leg pattern, a great way to burn physical energy in small spaces.
Tip for tough days: Use it before bed if the child struggles to 'power down' the body at night — the short, intense effort helps tire them out without over-stimulating.
Materials: A mat or rug
🎈Emotional Regulation

Walking the Line Backwards

Age: 6–10 yearsDuration: 3 minutes
A more challenging version of walking the line, done backwards, requiring even more balance and concentration.
  1. Use the same line from the previous activity.
  2. Have the child walk backwards along the line, slowly and carefully.
  3. Stay close for safety, since they can't see where they're going.
Why it works: The added difficulty demands even greater concentration, great for older children who already master the basic version.
Tip for tough days: Only introduce this version once the child is confident with the forward version, to avoid frustration.
Materials: A rope, tape, or a line drawn on the floor
🌬️Breathing & Focus

Box Breathing

Age: 7–10 yearsDuration: 2 to 3 minutes
The child breathes in counting to 4, holds the breath counting to 4, breathes out counting to 4 and holds empty counting to 4, repeating the cycle.
  1. Draw (or imagine) a square, one side for each stage of the breath.
  2. Guide the count: breathe in counting to 4, hold counting to 4, breathe out counting to 4, hold empty counting to 4.
  3. Repeat the full cycle 3 to 5 times.
Why it works: Used even by athletes and the military to regulate the nervous system under pressure, it's a powerful tool for older children with frequent anger outbursts.
Tip for tough days: Practice tracing the square with a finger in the air or on a surface — the physical movement helps keep the count's rhythm.
Materials: No materials needed (optional: a drawing of a square)

🔀Coordination

The Elephant

Age: 4–10 yearsDuration: 2 to 3 minutes
With the arm extended alongside the ear and knees slightly bent, the child draws a large 8 using the whole body, like an elephant's trunk.
  1. Have them bend the knees slightly and rest the arm against the ear, like a trunk.
  2. Using the whole body (not just the arm), trace a large 8 in the air while looking at the hand.
  3. Repeat for 3 to 4 loops and switch arms.
Why it works: The wide, whole-body movement offers a gentle vestibular stimulus, helping to organize the balance system of very agitated or very 'checked-out' children.
Tip for tough days: If the child feels dizzy, reduce the size of the movement and take breaks.
Materials: Open space
💪Strength & Proprioception

Paired Bicycle

Age: 5–10 yearsDuration: 3 minutes
Two children lie on their backs facing each other, soles of their feet touching, and pedal together.
  1. Lay the two children on their backs, facing each other, soles of their feet touching.
  2. Have them pedal together, trying to keep the same rhythm.
  3. Encourage them to look at each other and laugh during the movement.
Why it works: Beyond the motor benefit, syncing up with a partner trains reading the other person's rhythm, important for social skills and cooperation.
Tip for tough days: A great activity for reducing sibling rivalry — cooperating physically tends to open the door to cooperating in other parts of the day.
Materials: A mat or rug
🎈Emotional Regulation

Tree Pose

Age: 4–10 yearsDuration: 1 to 2 minutes per side
A yoga posture where the child rests the sole of one foot on the opposite leg, with hands joined above the head.
  1. Have the child stand and rest the sole of one foot against the inside of the other leg (calf or thigh).
  2. Join the hands above the head, like tree branches.
  3. Hold the position while breathing slowly, then switch legs.
Why it works: Combines physical balance with mindful breathing, one of the most-used postures in kids' yoga for emotional regulation.
Tip for tough days: If the child loses balance, it's fine to rest the lifted foot lower, on the shin instead of the thigh — what matters is the experience, not perfection.

Simon Says

Age: 3–10 yearsDuration: 5 to 8 minutes
The child should only follow the adult's commands when they start with the phrase 'Simon says'; commands without that phrase should be ignored.
